Statistical Inference for Explainable Boosting Regression
Anonymous Author
Anonymous Institution
Abstract
Explainable boosting machines (EBMs) are
popular “glass-box” models that maintain
accuracy by boosting univariate trees and
achieve explainability through clear visual-
izations of each feature’s effect. However, un-
like linear model coefficients, univariate EBM
trees lack statistical inference, making it hard
to know which features truly matter. We ad-
dress this gap using recent advances in statis-
tical inference for gradient boosting, provid-
ing methods for statistical inference as well
as end-to-end theoretical guarantees. Using
a moving average instead of a sum of boosted
trees (Boulevard regularization) allows the
boosting process to converge to a feature-
wise kernel ridge regression. This produces
asymptotically normal predictions that are
only quadratically worse than the minimax
rate for nonparametric Lipschitz regression.
We construct prediction intervals for the re-
sponse and confidence intervals for each uni-
variate predictor, enabling rigorous hypoth-
esis tests for feature importance and further
explainability within EBMs.

Modern machine learning methods have proved highly
successful, achieving impressively high performance
even for incredibly complicated prediction tasks. Still,
this comes at a cost. These methods have been cri-
tiqued for their lack of transparency, often described
as resulting in “black-box” models. Much work has
been done in an attempt to provide explanations for

While EBMs provide a clear graphical representation
for the effects of each feature, these are still point es-
timates. The central question we ask for uncertainty
quantification can be phrased as this: Given a model
design, if we collect a new dataset and retrain the
model, how different would our new prediction be? In
response to this question, vanilla EBMs (Lou et al.,
2012) proposed variance estimates via bootstrapping,
providing coarse uncertainty quantification, though at
the cost of more complex computation.
In contrast to EBMs, spline-based GAMs enjoy a
richer asymptotic theory. Wahba (1983) established
the asymptotic distribution of smoothing spline es-
timators via a Bayesian posterior covariance argu-
ment, Silverman (1985) developed the equivalent ker-
nel representation and convergence theory, and Nychka
(1988) analyzed the frequentist coverage of Bayesian

Despite this development Lou et al. (2012) argued for

ods is limited to small numbers of boosting rounds
in Bühlmann (2002) and extended to structurally con-
strained models including GAMs in Zhou and Hooker
(2022). Here we employ the Boulevard regularization
method (Zhou and Hooker, 2019), replacing sums of
trees with averages in the training process, which we
adapt to the additive model structure of EBMs. Boule-
vard, particularly in conjunction with subsampling,
naturally avoids overfitting; this both downweighs the
influence of initial trees and allows us to study the
model in the limit of infinite boosting rounds in which
we can produce theoretical results without the need to
select or constrain the number of rounds. In particu-
lar, our work makes the following contributions
• Asymptotic Theory. We establish a central
limit theorem for EBM predictors, showing that
the Boulevard-regularized ensemble converges to a
feature-wise kernel ridge regression. The resulting
predictions are asymptotically normal and achieve
rates only quadratically worse than the minimax
rate for Lipschitz regression.
• Frequentist Inference. By leveraging our pro-
posed CLT for baseline vector β and feature-wise
predictions f̂(k)
, we extend the heuristic bootstrap
to a set of inference toolkits: Formal confidence
intervals, prediction intervals and reproduction
intervals Zhou and Hooker (2019). This also al-
lows a direct test of variable importance following
the methods of Mentch and Hooker (2015). We
round out those propositions by numerical exper-
iments to back up the validity of our algorithms.
• Computational Efficiency. Unlike prior infer-
ential approaches for random forests and boost-
ing, we provide two methods to achieve speedups.
One exploits the Nyström approximation of
Musco and Musco (2017), while the other exploits
the fact that modern implementations of regres-
sion trees use histograms. Both allow for inference
in linear time in the number of datapoints.
